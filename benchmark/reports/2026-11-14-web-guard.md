# The web guard against an agent's load, 2026-11-14

> **Status:** Record · $0 (a scripted fake site; no real web session was used) · raw results: [`results/2026-11-14-web-guard`](../results/2026-11-14-web-guard/) · the decision it tests: [ADR-035](../../docs/DECISIONS.md) · code: `apps/desktop/src-tauri/src/websession/guard.rs`

**Question.** A web session drives a chat website with the user's own account (ADR-035). On 2026-11-13 DeepSeek suspended the owner's account after Pygg sent it 24 prompts of 2,000 to 6,000 tokens in 15 minutes. Does the per-site guard added after that hold its limits when a client pushes as hard as a coding agent would, and can anything switch it off?

**Answer.** Yes, in every run. Forty short questions fired by four clients at once reached the fake site **5 times** on a default site and **once** on DeepSeek; the rest were refused with a `Retry-After` before anything touched the page. Prompts the size of the ones that got the account suspended were refused outright (HTTP 413) on DeepSeek and four of six were refused elsewhere. After the site showed a usage-limit notice, **none** of the next 20 requests reached it, and the account rests for an hour. Replayed on a virtual clock for 24 hours, the suspended run's workload gets **4 prompts in the first 15 minutes** on a default site instead of 24, and **0** on DeepSeek. Nothing in the app, its settings or its environment turns the guard off; a daily limit set to 1,000 is held at 100 (30 on DeepSeek).

The cost of that safety: a web session is slow by design. At most 5 short questions in any 10 minutes, 15 an hour and 50 a day on a default site (3, 6 and 30 on DeepSeek), and no tools. It is for occasional questions, not for an agent loop.

## Method

**End to end** (`websession::tests::guard_bench_end_to_end`, ignored test):

```text
cargo test --release --lib websession::tests::guard_bench -- --ignored --nocapture --test-threads 1
```

- The real gateway, the real web-session endpoint, the guard and the store (SQLite in memory), with a scripted page (`Site`) in place of the site's window: each new chat answers the prompt typed into it, or from a chosen prompt on shows "You have reached your usage limit." The page records every prompt that reached it, with its time and length.
- The app's pace and back-off (`Timing::guarded()`: a 10 s base rest after each reply, 30 s on DeepSeek, and a 5-minute first back-off); only the page polling is fast.
- Requests go through the gateway as an OpenAI-format client sends them (`POST /v1/chat/completions`, model `<connection>/<model>`), and no client waits for a `Retry-After`.
- Two site profiles: the default limits (Muse, ChatGPT, Perplexity and Grok share them) and DeepSeek's lower ones.
- Three workloads per profile, each on a fresh store: a burst of 40 short questions from 4 clients; 10 long prompts in a row (8,000, 16,000 and 24,000 characters twice, as in the suspended run, then four just under the site's cap); and a usage-limit notice on the second prompt followed by 20 more questions.

**On a virtual clock** (`websession::tests::guard_bench_simulation`): the guard's own decision function (`guard::decide`) with the bookkeeping the web service does around it (the weight kept when a request goes, the rest after its reply, the day's request count), over 24 hours. The client asks again 2 seconds after each answer or refusal, and each reply takes 15 s. This shows the limits over hours and days, which a wall-clock run cannot.

Built from commit 9d2899f with the two benchmark tests added (committed with this report). Machine: Intel Core i7-8700K, Windows 11 Pro.

## Results: end to end

| Site profile | Workload | Requests | Reached the site | What the clients got | Retry-After |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | --- | --- |
| Default (Muse) | 40 short questions, 4 clients at once | 40 | **5** | 5 answers; 35 × 429 | 30–548 s |
| DeepSeek | 40 short questions, 4 clients at once | 40 | **1** | 1 answer; 39 × 429 | 30 s |
| Default (Muse) | 10 long prompts (8k–24k, then 11,900 chars) | 10 | **3** (8,000, 8,000, 11,900 chars) | 3 answers; 4 × 413 too long; 3 × 429 | 3,553 s |
| DeepSeek | 10 long prompts (8k–24k, then 3,900 chars) | 10 | **3** (3,900 chars each) | 3 answers; 6 × 413 too long; 1 × 429 | 525 s |
| Default (Muse) | usage-limit notice on prompt 2, then 20 more | 22 | **2** | 1 answer; the notice as a 429 `insufficient_quota`; 20 × 429 | 3,600 s |
| DeepSeek | usage-limit notice on prompt 2, then 20 more | 22 | **2** | the same | 3,600 s |

- **No burst.** The shortest gap between two prompts that reached the site was 10.5 s on the default profile and 30.6 s on DeepSeek, the base rest; on DeepSeek the burst ended after one prompt because the next rest (30 s plus reading time) is longer than a request may wait in line (30 s).
- **Prompt-size caps.** 12,000 characters by default, 4,000 on DeepSeek. Refused prompts never reached the page.
- **Back-off after the site's own warning.** The usage-limit notice became an error for the client (not an answer), and set a back-off of at least one hour (`Trouble` floor 12 × 5 minutes); the next 20 requests were refused by the gateway without reaching the endpoint.
- **Ceilings.** A daily request limit set to 1,000 is in force as 100 on the default profile and 30 on DeepSeek.

## Can it be switched off?

No, as far as this code goes:

- Every limit is a constant per site (`guard::limits`), not a setting. The one number a user sets, the daily request limit, is held under the site's ceiling (`Limits::daily_limit`).
- The web service holds its timing at or above the guard's floors outside the crate's own tests (`Service::new` applies `Timing::guarded()` under `#[cfg(not(test))]`); the app builds it with `Timing::default()`.
- No environment variable reaches the guard: the only one read under `src/websession/` is `PYGG_WEB_DEVTOOLS`, which opens the browser's developer tools in debug builds.
- The decision is made from what Pygg recorded in its store (`web_pace`, `providers.web_*`), so restarting Pygg does not reset it.

## What this does not show

- **A real site's thresholds.** No site publishes the pace that gets an account suspended. These runs show that Pygg keeps the limits it sets, not that those limits are low enough for every site. DeepSeek's lower limits are a reaction to one suspension, not a measured safe rate.
- **The page itself.** The fake page answers instantly and always in the expected shape; a real site's page changes, slows down and shows checks, which the other web-session tests cover with scripted pages. No real web session was run for this report, on purpose (the owner's accounts are protected; see ADR-035).
- **Other projects' behaviour in practice.** Their rows are from code and documentation read on one day; none was run against a fake site here.
